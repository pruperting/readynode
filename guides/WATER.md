# Drinking water when the taps stop

## Before a forecast outage

Fill the bath and your containers, and keep any hot water cylinder topped up. Keep a water butt lidded so it doesn't grow algae or breed mosquitoes.

## Where to get water, in order

Use the first source available. Rivers and streams come last, for good reason.

1. **Stored bottled water.** 3 litres per person per day: 36 L covers four people for three days.
2. **Water already in the house.**
   - A **hot water cylinder** holds roughly 100-200 L: about two weeks of drinking water for four people. It's mains water that was treated before it went in. Boil it before drinking. To drain it safely: switch off the boiler and any immersion heater first (an immersion heater in an emptying tank burns out); let it cool (it's stored at around 60°C); close the cold feed so nothing dirty is drawn in, and if it's an **unvented** (pressurised) cylinder, open a hot tap to release the pressure; then drain into clean containers from the drain valve near the bottom, with a hose.
   - A **cold water tank in the loft** is fine for washing and flushing. Treat it before drinking, because loft tanks are often open to dust and insects.
   - A **combi boiler** stores none of this. Check which you have now, before you need it.
   - The **toilet cistern** (not the bowl) is fine for washing.
3. **Official supply.** Water companies must provide at least **10 litres per person per day** during a supply failure, starting within the first 24 hours (the Security and Emergency Measures Direction). Water companies announce bottled water stations and bowsers on their websites and local radio. Take the containers from your kit.
4. **Rainwater** from a water butt. Use it first for **flushing toilets** (a bucket down the pan) and washing, so stored water is kept for drinking. Roof water is far cleaner than river water: no sewage, no farm runoff. A **first-flush diverter** on the downpipe dumps the dirtiest first few litres off the roof. As a rough guide, 5 mm of rain on 20 m² of roof feeding one downpipe gives about 100 L.
5. **Streams and rivers.** Usable with treatment, but only if nothing better is available.

## Why rivers come last

Filters and boiling deal with **germs**. They don't deal with **chemicals**, and UK rivers carry both:

- **Sewage from storm overflows.** These discharge into rivers in heavy rain, which is when floods and many emergencies happen. Most UK rivers near towns have them.
- **Farm runoff**: slurry, fertiliser, pesticides.
- **Road runoff**: fuel, oil and metals, especially after heavy rain.
- **Floodwater**, which can carry all of the above.

**Choosing a spot**

- Upstream of houses, roads, farm buildings and overflow outfalls.
- Running water, not ponds or still edges.
- Clear water. Avoid it after heavy rain if you can wait.
- No oily sheen, foam, or chemical or sewage smell. If you see any of these, don't use that water at all; treatment won't fix it.
- While you still have internet, look up the storm overflow map for whichever stream you'd use, and note where the outfalls are.

## How to treat collected water

Do every step, every time.

1. **Settle.** Leave it in a bucket for an hour or two (overnight if it's cloudy). Pour off the clearer water from the top.
2. **Pre-filter.** Pour it through a clean cloth, a coffee filter or a pillowcase to take out silt. This stops the filter clogging.
3. **Filter.** A hollow-fibre filter (Sawyer Squeeze or similar, 0.1 micron) removes bacteria and parasites, including Cryptosporidium and Giardia, which chlorine handles badly. It **doesn't remove viruses or chemicals**.
4. **Disinfect.** This kills the viruses the filter lets through. Use one of these:
   - **Boil.** Bring it to a rolling boil and keep it there for **1 minute**. This is the most reliable method, but it needs fuel.
   - **Chlorine dioxide tablets.** Follow the pack's dose and waiting time; it's longer for cold or cloudy water.
   - **NaDCC tablets (e.g. Aquatabs).** Cheap and long-lasting. Wait the full time on the pack.
5. **Store.** Use clean, lidded, food-grade containers, labelled "treated" with the date. Use untreated-but-boiled water within a day.

**Optional:** an activated carbon filter improves taste and removes some chemicals. It doesn't make chemically polluted water safe, so the rules for choosing a source still apply.

## About bleach and pool shock

- **Household bleach**: avoid it. UK bleaches usually contain thickeners, fragrance or detergent, and strengths vary. Tablets are safer and easier to dose.
- **Pool shock (calcium hypochlorite)** keeps for years, as a backup. Never add it straight to drinking water; make a stock solution first. The US EPA method: dissolve 1 heaped teaspoon (about 7 g) in 7.5 L of water. Then add 1 part of that stock to 100 parts of water (100 ml per 10 L) and wait 30 minutes. It's a strong oxidiser, so store it dry, sealed, and away from fuel, ammonia and anything flammable. Keep it out of reach.

## Kit worth having

- Hollow-fibre filter, kept indoors so it never freezes (frost damage is invisible and ruins it)
- Purification tablets, about 50
- 2 food-grade containers, 10-20 L, with taps
- Pre-filter cloths or coffee filters
- A water butt on a downpipe, ideally with a first-flush diverter
- A way to boil: camping stove and spare gas, or a Kelly kettle that burns twigs
- A spare clean bucket kept only for water

## Practise once

Treat a bucket of rainwater from start to finish. You'll find out how long it takes, whether the filter fits your containers, and what's missing. It's much easier to learn on a dry Sunday than during an emergency.
